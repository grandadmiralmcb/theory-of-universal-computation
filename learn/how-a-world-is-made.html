A build-up from four ideas

How a World Is Made

This is a short course in a theory that starts with almost nothing and tries to get to everything. It begins with four assumptions, and then adds one small machine at a time. Nothing here needs mathematics beyond counting, and each step can be worked by hand before you read what it means.

Nine stepsEight machines you can operateCounting only

Step 00 Before anything else

Why is there anything to look at?

The theory starts with four assumptions and tries to earn everything else. Before meeting them it is worth asking the awkward question, because the theory has to survive it.

The rule that drives everything here is that when the world has a choice, it takes whichever option disturbs the least. Nothing at all would seem to disturb nothing at all. So why has the world not simply stopped?

The answer is that the rule compares moves, not situations. A situation sitting still has no cost to compare. Cost is charged to a change, so “nothing is cheapest” is not a losing option, it is not an option at all. And there is no move that would get you there: the moves available rearrange what exists, and none of them empties the world.

So the honest position is a split, and this course will keep pointing at splits like this one.

ClaimStatus
There is something rather than nothingAssumed
Once there is something, it cannot become nothingFollows

The theory does not explain why there is anything. It explains why, given anything at all, there is always something. That is less than you might want and more than most starting points manage, and pretending otherwise would spoil the rest of the course.

How to read the labels

Three labels appear throughout. Follows means it comes from the four assumptions and nothing else. Assumed means something extra was added, and you will always be told what. Not claimed means the theory says plainly that it has nothing to offer.

Step 01 The first machine

Telling two things apart

The smallest thing that can happen is that two things are compared and found to be the same, or not. That comparison is the first machine, and it is the only machine that makes something out of nothing but a relation.

Put two things in. One light comes out.

This matters more than it looks. People often say the world is made of ones and zeros. But a one or a zero cannot exist by itself. To have a bit you need two things that could be told apart, something doing the telling, and an answer. Those requirements are not built out of bits; bits are built out of them.

Count how many comparisons it takes to pin down which of several possibilities you are looking at, and you have rebuilt the ordinary measure of information. It arrives at the end of the story rather than the beginning.

Step 02 The one that changes everything

Holding the same thing, or holding a copy

Here are two machines that look almost identical and are not. Getting the difference between them is most of the work of this course; everything later depends on it.

On the left, one thing is held by two holders. There is one of it. On the right, a copier has made a second thing, so there are two.

They behave the same until something is cut.

Why a theory would want this

You might think a theory would prefer copies, since they are simpler. But if everything were copies, nothing anywhere could be connected to anything else, and the world would be a heap of separate pieces with no relationships in it. Every interesting thing in physics — and every interesting thing in this course — happens because two parts of the world can hold something in common.

Step 03 Machines that do work

Holes, plugs, and things happening

So far nothing has moved. Two more machines fix that.

A punch takes a structure and removes a piece, leaving a hole. A plug is a piece that fits a hole. When a hole and a matching plug end up facing each other, the arrangement can be completed — and completing it is the only kind of event this theory has.

This is the point at which the theory quietly says something large. It does not describe things happening in time. It says the happening is the time. If nothing were being fitted anywhere, there would be no time passing, because there would be nothing for time to be made of.

Step 04 A line with two ends

Order arrives, but direction does not

Lay those events out in the sequence they happened and you get a line. This is the only picture in the course that needs just one dimension, and there is a reason to draw it plainly.

Step 05 Choosing

The world has preferences

When more than one fitting is available, which one happens? The theory's answer is that the world takes whichever disturbs the least, and it measures disturbance by counting three things.

CountWhat it counts
Junctions brokenShared things you had to cut apart.
Holes openedLoose ends you left behind.
Lamp changedWhether the comparator from step 01 now gives a different answer.

Put two candidate moves on a balance and the cheaper one wins. That is the whole of the dynamics. There is no force anywhere in this theory, only a preference.

That distinction between a latch and a weight is worth keeping. It is the difference between a law that is never broken and a tendency that can be outweighed, and in this theory the two are not different kinds of thing. They are the same kind of thing at different heights. What we call conservation laws sit at latch height. What we call forces sit on the scale.

It is also the point where this stops being physics and starts being something more general. A preference ordering over available options, with some options ruled out absolutely and the rest traded off, is the shape of a theory of value. This one just happens to belong to the world rather than to a person in it.

Step 06 The one-way move

Which end is the past

Almost everything that happens can be undone. A fitting can be unfitted; the arrangement goes back to what it was and nothing about the reversed film looks wrong. Those events carry no direction at all, which is what step 04 was showing.

One kind of event is different. Sometimes the world holds several possibilities at once, joined by junctions, and then commits to one of them by cutting the rest loose. The pieces cut loose do not vanish. They are still there. They are simply no longer reachable from where you now are, and there is no move that reaches back.

Three things turn out to be the same event here, which is unusual enough to be worth noticing. The direction of time, the fact that disorder grows rather than shrinks, and the moment a measurement in physics gets a definite answer are one event looked at three ways.

It also explains something you have seen without being told why. Time obviously flows for a cup that breaks and does not obviously flow at all for a single atom left alone. Committing happens constantly for large things in contact with their surroundings and almost never for small isolated ones. Direction is as common as the world's habit of settling on one definite outcome, and those are not two facts.

Step 07 Counting a boundary

Only the edge matters

Now a result rather than a machine, and it is the one everything later leans on.

Suppose you want to separate some part of the world from the rest. You will have to cut every junction that crosses the line you have drawn. Junctions buried inside are untouched: both their ends are inside with you. So the price of separating something depends on its boundary and not on its contents.

That last point is easy to skip past and is the reason this figure is drawn the way it is. The result is about a boundary in the sense of “what did you have to cut”, not a boundary in the sense of “a surface with an area in square metres”. So far the theory has never mentioned space at all.

Step 08 The step that costs something

Where space is assumed

Everything up to here followed from four assumptions and some counting, and the pictures stayed flat because nothing needed more than a flat picture.

To get physics — actual space, distances, areas, gravity — one more thing is needed, and the theory cannot currently supply it. It has to be assumed. The assumption is that the diagram we have been drawing, when it gets very large, settles down into something with a shape: a place where distances mean something and a boundary has an area.

Press the button and watch the assumption being made. Nothing is added to the diagram. It is the same diagram. It is given a dimension.

Past the button, a lot follows quickly, and it is worth stating briefly rather than in detail. Being hard to push and being pulled hard by gravity turn out to be the same count of junctions, which is why a hammer and a feather fall together. Boundaries you cannot reach back across — black holes — can only grow, so two of them merging must make a bigger one. And the strength of gravity stops being a bare number and becomes a measure of how densely the empty world is stitched together.

The honest position on all of that

Each of those results is real work and each is Assumed, resting on the button you just pressed. Nobody has shown that the diagram does settle into a shape. Until someone does, this part of the theory is a careful account of what would follow if it did.

If one thing from this course is worth keeping it is probably the shape of that table rather than any single result before it. Being able to say exactly which of your claims are earned, which are borrowed, and which are absent is a habit that works on any theory, including the ones you meet elsewhere.

What the machines are made of

A fair objection to the whole course: if everything is made of comparators and junctions and plugs, what are those made of? The answer is nothing, and that is not a dodge. The machines are a way of drawing relationships, chosen because you already have intuitions about how machines behave. Other drawings work equally well and mathematicians use several. What the theory says is real is whatever stays the same when you change the drawing — which is exactly why the labels in this course matter more than the pictures.

Every result in steps 05 to 08 has a short program attached that checks it and prints the numbers. They need only Python, with nothing to install.

The theory itself is written up in a set of numbered documents. The labels used in this course — follows, assumed, not claimed — are its own, taken from its charter.
